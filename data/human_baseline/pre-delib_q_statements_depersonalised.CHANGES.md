# Depersonalised Q-statements — what was changed and why

Source: `pre-delib_discourse_mapping_factor_array.csv` (OP12 Table 2, 46 items).
Output: `pre-delib_q_statements_depersonalised.csv`, which holds **both** forms
side by side. Generated by `scripts/build_depersonalised_statements.py`.

## Why depersonalise at all

Two thirds of the Q-statements are phrased as attitude reports — "I'm concerned
that X", "I think X", "it makes me sick to hear X" — rather than as claims about
the world. For a Q-sort that is unremarkable: the participant is asked how much
the card sounds like them. For automated stance scoring it is fatal.

The essays we score are largely reported speech (53.5% of paragraphs contain an
attributed-view construction such as "some argue" or "critics contend"). Against
an attitude-report hypothesis, an NLI model correctly returns *neutral*, because
"many Australians worry that X" genuinely does not entail "**I** am concerned
that X". The measurement then reports nothing at all.

Measured on three test cases (`src/validate_stance.py`):

| Hypothesis form | Mean voiced-minus-dismissed gap |
|---|---|
| Original (attitude report) | **−0.294** (wrong direction) |
| Depersonalised | **+0.789** |

## The justification

A participant placing "I'm concerned that X" at +2 is expressing agreement with
the proposition that X is worrying. The attitude wrapper is a survey convention;
the z-score attaches to the content inside it. Removing the wrapper recovers
what the sort measured rather than altering it.

This argument is strong for the `stripped` cases and weaker for the `recast`
cases below, which is why they are listed individually.

## Both forms are retained

Nothing is discarded. `item_original` and `item_depersonalised` sit in adjacent
columns, every scorer runs against both, and results are reported for each.
Where they disagree, that disagreement is a finding about the instrument, not an
error to be resolved.

## Classification

| Class | n | What was done |
|---|---|---|
| `unchanged` | 21 | Already a proposition; copied verbatim |
| `stripped` | 16 | Attitude wrapper removed, proposition otherwise intact |
| `recast` | 7 | Wrapper removal required rewording that is **not** meaning-neutral |
| `repaired` | 2 | Grammar only |

Curly apostrophes were normalised to straight throughout, in both columns.

### The two repairs

**1.** "Mitochondrial donation doesn't fundamentally change our genetics should
be viewed like any other organ transplant." — missing conjunction in the
published table; "genetics **and** should be viewed" inserted. The original is
ungrammatical as printed.

**28.** "Genome editing technologies **gives** us power" → "**give**".

### The seven recast cases

These are the ones to argue about. In each, stripping the wrapper left no
grammatical proposition behind, so the claim had to be reconstructed — and
reconstruction imports a reading.

**6.** *"I think it would be amazing to have a choice in the way someone
behaves."* → *"Being able to choose the way someone behaves would be a good
thing."*
"Amazing" is flattened to "a good thing". The original's enthusiasm is lost;
the evaluative direction is preserved.

**19.** *"I have a very different feeling about gene editing in humans than I do
for animals or plants."* → *"Gene editing in humans is fundamentally different
from gene editing in animals or plants."*
This is the largest shift. A report of differential *feeling* becomes a claim
about a difference in *kind*. Someone could agree that humans feel different to
them while denying any principled distinction. Treat results on this item with
caution.

**21.** *"My cultural beliefs make me cautious about genome editing."* →
*"Cultural beliefs are a reason to be cautious about genome editing."*
A personal report becomes a general normative claim. The original is only about
the speaker; the rewrite makes a claim about everyone. This matters because item
21 is one of the strongest discriminators between A/B (−1.81, −1.50) and C
(+1.35).

**24.** *"Anything that's been artificially created in a lab gives me
Frankenstein vibes."* → *"Anything that has been artificially created in a lab
is unnatural and unsettling."*
Idiom replaced with its literal content. "Frankenstein vibes" carries a
cultural-horror register that "unsettling" does not.

**29.** *"It makes me sick to hear that genome editing will 'cure' disability."*
→ *"It is offensive to speak of genome editing as a 'cure' for disability."*
Visceral reaction becomes a normative judgement. The scare quotes around 'cure'
are retained, since they carry the disability-rights argument that is the point
of the item.

**35.** *"I would strongly consider using genome editing for my children if it
could safely give them an advantage in life."* → *"It is acceptable for parents
to use genome editing if it could safely give their children an advantage in
life."*
Personal willingness becomes general permissibility. A participant could think
it acceptable for others but not for themselves, or the reverse.

**38.** *"I would be OK with having my own genome edited."* → *"It is acceptable
for a person to have their own genome edited."*
Same shift as 35, applied to the speaker's own body. Note that 35 and 38 were
distinct items precisely because self and offspring differ; both rewrites move
them towards the same general claim, which risks collapsing a distinction the
instrument drew deliberately.

## Known limitation

The recast items concentrate in the discourse-C direction (21, 24, 29 are all
items where C scores high). If the depersonalised run shifts C's share relative
to the original run, suspect this before believing it is a real effect.
